Stable and unstable manifolds on spheres

Compare height on a round sphere with height on a deformed sphere. The round sphere has a maximum and a minimum. The deformation adds a second maximum and a saddle. Both examples use downward gradient flow for the metric induced from Euclidean space.

Stable and unstable: the convention

Let Φt be the flow of −∇f, where f is height. The stable manifold consists of points that approach the selected critical point in forward time; the unstable manifold consists of points that approach it in backward time.

Ws(p)={q:limt→+∞Φt(q)=p}, Wu(p)={q:limt→−∞Φt(q)=p}.

Each manifold includes its own critical point. On a surface, a critical point of Morse index λ has stable dimension 2 − λ and unstable dimension λ. A shaded region represents a two-dimensional manifold; the curves drawn within it are sample trajectories.

The round sphere

For height on the unit sphere, the north pole d is the maximum and the south pole a is the minimum. Every nonconstant trajectory follows a meridian from d toward a. There are no saddles.

Both two-dimensional manifolds are spheres with one point removed.

The deformed sphere

The left peak d and right peak c are maxima; b is the saddle in the notch, and a is the minimum. The stable manifold of b has two branches, flowing from d and c toward b. Its unstable manifold has two branches, one on the front and one on the back, both flowing toward a.

Let C be the closed upper arc from d through b to c. Let Γ be the closed loop formed by the two descending saddle branches together with a and b. This loop separates the surface Σ into two open regions: Dc containing c, and Dd containing d.

Each two-dimensional manifold is an open disk. Selecting a maximum shades the points that approach it in backward time. Selecting the minimum shades everything except C; the excluded arc is drawn in a neutral color. The critical-point count is 1 − 1 + 2 = 2, the Euler characteristic of a sphere.

These sphere flows are Morse–Smale: their stable and unstable manifolds meet transversely. In the deformed example there is only one saddle, and its stable and unstable curves cross transversely at b. The two descending branches both end at a minimum. Compare this with the saddle-to-saddle connections in the torus example.

Surface model and gradient equations

Starting with x2 + y2 + z2 = 1, use the embedding

F(x,y,z)=(x,y,z+Ax2(1+z)3+Bx(1+z)).

The round sphere uses A = B = 0. The deformed sphere uses A = 1/2 and B = −3/20. The factors of 1 + z concentrate the deformation toward the top, keeping the lower body round. Height f is the third displayed coordinate.

Set K = 2Ax(1 + z)3 + B(1 + z), L = 1 + 3Ax2(1 + z)2 + Bx, h = Lx − Kz, and D = h2 + L2y2 + z2. The induced negative-gradient flow, written in the original unit-sphere coordinates, is

x˙=zhD, y˙=zLyD, z˙=−xh−Ly2D.

This vector field is tangent to the unit sphere, and along it ḟ = −(h2 + L2y2)/D ≤ 0. The surface is rendered from this embedding, and the displayed trajectories are numerical approximations to its gradient curves.

The vertical derivative of F is L ≥ 0.85 throughout the closed unit ball. Thus the deformation is injective with invertible derivative there, and its boundary remains a smooth embedded sphere.
